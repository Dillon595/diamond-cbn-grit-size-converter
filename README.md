## Why Grit Size Matters

Grit size is one of the key variables in grinding wheel selection.

In general:

- Coarser abrasive can support higher stock removal.
- Finer abrasive can support finer finishing.
- Actual grinding performance also depends on bond, concentration, wheel speed, machine stiffness, workpiece material, dressing, and coolant.

Therefore, grit size should not be selected independently from the rest of the wheel specification.

## Diamond Grit

Diamond abrasives are widely used for applications involving hard and brittle materials such as carbide, ceramics, PCD, PCBN, glass, ferrite, and graphite.

Common diamond grit designations include D-series sizes such as D20, D46, D64 and D91.

The exact particle-size distribution depends on the applicable grading standard and supplier specification.

## CBN Grit

CBN is widely used in grinding applications involving hardened steels and other ferrous materials.

CBN grit can be specified using different grading systems, and direct conversion between standards should be treated as approximate unless the applicable standard provides an exact equivalence.

## Important Note About Conversion

A grit number is not necessarily a universal particle-size measurement.

Different abrasive standards may classify particles differently. Therefore, the converter labels results as approximate where appropriate rather than pretending that every grit system has a perfect one-to-one equivalent.

## Common Questions

### How do I convert diamond grit to microns?

Diamond grit can be related to an approximate particle-size range, but the exact relationship depends on the grading standard. The tool provides a reference range rather than claiming universal equivalence.

### What does D46 mean?

D46 is a commonly used diamond grit designation associated with an approximate particle-size class. The exact distribution depends on the applicable grading specification.

### What is the difference between D46 and F46?

D and F designations can refer to different abrasive classification systems. They should not automatically be treated as identical simply because the number is the same.

### Is mesh the same as FEPA grit?

No. Mesh and FEPA are different classification approaches. A rough comparison may be possible, but exact interchangeability should not be assumed.
